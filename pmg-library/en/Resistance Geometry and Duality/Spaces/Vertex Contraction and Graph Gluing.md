Suppose two vertices of a graph are to be treated as one. For example, several objects may be combined into a single group. How will the couplings and effective resistances change? What space will correspond to the new graph?

Consider another problem as well. Two graphs are given independently. Select one vertex in each and identify them. The resulting graph allows us to compare vertices from the two original parts. Here, identification joins two spaces.

It uses the [[Basis|distinction between elements and affine vectors]], [[Decomposing a Form into Atomic and Difference Parts|orthogonal projection]], and the [[Laplacian Exponential|metric polyform]]. All existing couplings have positive strengths. Connectedness is assumed wherever the resistance metric of an entire graph is calculated.

## Three ways to reduce the number of vertices

Start with a triangle $a,b,c$ whose coupling strengths are all $1$. The effective resistance between each pair of vertices is $2/3$. In resistance geometry, this is an equilateral triangle with side length $\sqrt{2/3}$.

Let us retain two objects instead of three. The result depends on the operation we choose.

| Operation | Result | Effective resistance or squared distance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Select the affine line through $a,b$ | A subspace with the restriction of the original metric | $R_{ab}=2/3$ |
| Delete vertex $c$ and both its couplings | A graph with one coupling of coefficient $1$ | $R_{ab}=1$ |
| Identify $b,c$ as a vertex $s$ | The coefficients of $ab,ac$ add; the coupling $bc$ disappears | $R_{as}=1/2$ |

![Three Ways to Obtain Two Vertices](<images/Three Ways to Obtain Two Vertices.svg>)

*Figure 1. In the first case, the metric of the selected vertices is preserved. In the other two, the metric is calculated for the transformed graph. Numbers beside lines denote coupling strengths; the dashed line marks the selected affine line.*

Deleting a vertex removes its couplings. Contracting vertices transfers their couplings to the common vertex. Neither operation is the same as simply selecting the subspace generated by the remaining vertices. Eliminating internal vertices while preserving the metric of selected vertices will be a separate problem later in the series.

The [[en/PythonExamples/Example - Subspaces and Graph Transformations#Deletion, contraction, and elimination|Python example]] reproduces this calculation.

## Vertex identification in polyform algebra

> [!info] Vertex identification in polyform algebra
> **Definition.** Contracting vertices $a,b$ means replacing them with a single vertex $s$. A coupling $ab$ need not exist. Define a linear map $\varphi$ on the basis vertices by
>
> $$
> \varphi(a)=\varphi(b)=s,\qquad \varphi(x)=x\quad(x\ne a,b)
> $$

Linearity means that the image of a combination is obtained by replacing each element while retaining its coefficient. In particular, for $d=(ab)=b-a$, we have $\varphi(d)=0$.

The map preserves affine weight: elements map to elements, and affine vectors to affine vectors. It extends to exterior products and forms by

$$
\varphi(X  Y)=\varphi(X) \varphi(Y),\qquad
\varphi([X,Y])=[\varphi(X),\varphi(Y)]
$$

It also commutes with the boundary operator: $\varphi(\partial X)=\partial\varphi(X)$. For example, the boundary $(abc)$ vanishes after identifying $a,b$, while $(acx)$ becomes $(scx)$.

Write $L'$ for the Laplacian of the new graph $G'$, $N$ for its exponential, and $M$ for the original exponential. Then

$$
\boxed{L'=\varphi(L_G),\qquad N=\varphi(M)}
\tag{1}
$$

Indeed, the form of the coupling $ab$ becomes zero. The couplings $ax,bx$ map to the same form $[(sx)]^2$, with coefficient $c_{ax}+c_{bx}$. The extension of $\varphi$ preserves products of forms, so it maps $\exp L_G$ to $\exp\varphi(L_G)$.

> [!example] Contracting the endpoints of a path
> Take the path $a-b-c$ with unit coupling strengths. Its metric polyform is
>
> $$
> M=e+[(ab)]^2+[(bc)]^2+[(abc)]^2
> $$
>
> Identify $a,c$ as $s$. The last term vanishes, and the two coupling forms coincide:
>
> $$
> L'=2[(sb)]^2,\qquad N=e+2[(sb)]^2
> $$
>
> The resulting graph has one coupling of coefficient $2$. Its effective resistance is $1/2$, and its spanning-tree coefficient is $2$.

Thus contraction acts on the arguments of forms themselves.

## Which subspace does contraction produce?

Assume the original graph is connected. Let $W$ denote its space of affine vectors, and let

$$
S=\{t d:t\in\mathbb R\},\qquad d=b-a
$$

be the direction that disappears under contraction. The vectors $v$ and $v+td$ have the same image. Algebraically, the new space is therefore the **quotient space** $W/S$: each of its elements is a class of vectors that differ by an element of $S$.

The metric allows us to represent this quotient space within the original $W$. Write

$$
S^\perp=\{v\in W:v\cdot d=0\}
$$

The symbol $S^\perp$ denotes the orthogonal complement: the space of vectors perpendicular to every vector in $S$. Each class $v+S$ has a unique representative in $S^\perp$:

$$
v_\perp=v-\frac{v\cdot d}{d^2}d
\tag{2}
$$

Here $d^2=\|d\|^2=R_{ab}>0$ is calculated in the original graph.

> [!info] The metric after contraction
> **Theorem.** The space of affine vectors of $G'$ is isometric to $S^\perp$: the correspondence $\varphi(v)\leftrightarrow v_\perp$ preserves inner products. In particular,
>
> $$
> \boxed{\|\varphi(v)\|_{G'}^2=v^2-\frac{(v\cdot d)^2}{d^2}=\|v_\perp\|_G^2}
> \tag{3}
> $$

This is the precise sense in which contraction gives a passage to a subspace. The subspace is the orthogonal complement of the disappearing direction, rather than the span of an arbitrary set of original vertices retained as representatives. The dimension decreases by one.

### Derivation using potentials

Recall that $\tau(G)$ is the spanning-tree coefficient, and $u_M(f)$ is the coefficient of the original spanning-tree form in the product $Mf$. The metric identity in the [[Core specification#Potential and the metric identity|core specification]] identifies the normalized potential with the numerical metric evaluation of the form.

> [!info] Derivation using potentials
> **Lemma.** For the contraction of a pair $a,b$,
>
> $$
> \tau(G')=u_M([d]^2)=\tau(G)d^2
> \tag{4}
> $$
>
> $$
> u_N([\varphi(v)]^2)=u_M([d]^2[v]^2)
> \tag{5}
> $$
>
> On the left of (5), the spanning-tree coefficient is extracted on the new vertex set; on the right, it is extracted on the original set.

> [!note]- Proof of the lemma and theorem
> **Proof.** Write the original vertices as $a,b,x_1,\ldots,x_{n-2}$ and choose the vector basis $d=b-a$, $v_i=x_i-a$. After contraction, the images $\varphi(v_i)=x_i-s$ remain.
>
> In grade $n-2$, substitution by $\varphi$ annihilates terms containing $d$ in at least one argument of a form. Multiplication by $[d]^2$ annihilates the same terms. For all remaining terms, the coefficient of the new spanning-tree form equals the coefficient of the original spanning-tree form after multiplication by $[d]^2$: the product $d  v_1 \cdots  v_{n-2}$ is the boundary $(abx_1\ldots x_{n-2})$.
>
> Apply this rule to $M$ and to $M[v]^2$. Together with (1), it gives (4) and (5). When the original graph has two vertices, the new spanning-tree form is $e$, and the rule still holds.
>
> Divide (5) by (4) and apply the metric identity:
>
> $$
> \|\varphi(v)\|_{G'}^2
> =\frac{u_M([d]^2[v]^2)}{u_M([d]^2)}
> =\frac{d^2v^2-(d\cdot v)^2}{d^2}
> $$
>
> The numerator is the Gram determinant of the two vectors. This gives (3). Polarizing the equality of norms gives preservation of inner products. Finally, (2) selects the unique representative of each class in $S^\perp$, proving the isometry. $\square$

The product $M[d]^2$ in (4) and (5) is used to extract potentials in the original space. The metric polyform of the new graph itself is given by substitution (1).

> [!example] Geometry of the contracted path
> Return to the path $a-b-c$. The disappearing direction is now $d=c-a$, and $v=b-a$. In the original metric,
>
> $$
> d^2=2,\qquad v^2=1,\qquad v\cdot d=1
> $$
>
> Hence $\|\varphi(v)\|^2=1-1/2=1/2$, in agreement with the direct calculation for the new graph. Formula (4) gives $\tau(G')=1\cdot2=2$.

![Contraction as Projection](<images/Contraction as Projection.svg>)

*Figure 2. The resistance realization of the unit path is a right triangle. Projection onto the altitude from $b$ identifies $a,c$ at their midpoint $s$. The squared length of $bs$ is $1/2$.*

If several groups of vertices are identified, $S$ consists of all linear combinations of differences within these groups. Successive applications of (3) realize the new space as $S^\perp$. Its dimension is $n-1-\dim S$, independently of the order of contractions. For example, in a path on four vertices, identifying the first two and the last two leaves one coupling between the two groups and a one-dimensional space.

## Independent components and the top-grade form

A disconnected graph also has a common formal space of vertices. A finite resistance metric, however, is defined separately within each connected component. The published core does not assign a finite resistance norm to a difference of vertices from distinct components.

This distinction appears directly in the Laplacian exponential. Suppose a graph on $n$ vertices has $c$ connected components $G_1,\ldots,G_c$. Then

$$
L_G=\sum_{\alpha=1}^c L_\alpha,\qquad
M=\prod_{\alpha=1}^c M_\alpha
$$

Let $n_\alpha$ be the number of vertices in a component, let $\Omega_\alpha$ be the boundary of the simplex on all its vertices, and let $\tau_\alpha=\tau(G_\alpha)$. The highest nonzero component of the exponential is

$$
\boxed{(M)_{n-c}=\left(\prod_{\alpha=1}^c\tau_\alpha\right)
[\Omega_1 \cdots \Omega_c]^2}
\tag{6}
$$

Each factor $\Omega_\alpha$ describes the space of internal vectors of its component, of dimension $n_\alpha-1$. The highest grade is therefore the sum of these dimensions: $n-c$. When the number of vertices is known, the number of independent components is recovered as $c=n-k_{\max}$, where $k_{\max}$ is the highest nonzero grade.

**Remark.** For an isolated vertex $a$, we have $\Omega=\partial a=1$ and take $\tau=1$. Such a factor is invisible in the product. The number of components therefore cannot be found merely by counting the displayed factors without keeping track of the original vertex set. When $c>1$, the coefficient $\tau(G)$ of the form of grade $n-1$ is zero; the product $\prod_\alpha\tau_\alpha$ in (6) belongs to a different grade.

> [!example] Two independent couplings
> For unit couplings $ab$ and $cd$,
>
> $$
> M=e+[(ab)]^2+[(cd)]^2+[(ab) (cd)]^2
> $$
>
> The highest grade is $2$. With $n=4$, we obtain two components. Adding an isolated vertex does not change the polyform, but now $n=5$ and there are three components.

## Gluing at a single vertex

> [!info] Gluing at a single vertex
> **Definition.** Select vertices $s_1\in G_1$, $s_2\in G_2$ in two initially disjoint connected graphs. Identifying them as a common vertex $s$ is called gluing at a single vertex. For now, there are no other identifications or new couplings.

After substituting the vertex names, denote the polyforms of the parts by $M_1,M_2$.

Denote their potentials by $u_1(f)$ and $u_2(f)$: they extract the coefficients of the respective spanning-tree forms from $M_1f$ and $M_2f$. We obtain

$$
L_G=L_1+L_2,\qquad M=M_1M_2
$$

Unlike contraction within a connected component, this operation removes no internal direction of either part. If the parts had $n_1,n_2$ vertices, the dimension of the new graph's space is

$$
(n_1+n_2-1)-1=(n_1-1)+(n_2-1)
$$

Exterior products show how two components become one. Put the common vertex first in both boundaries. The merging rule gives

$$
(sa_1\ldots a_r) (sb_1\ldots b_s)
=(sa_1\ldots a_r b_1\ldots b_s)
$$

The top-grade forms of the parts multiply to give the top-grade form of the entire graph. Hence

$$
\boxed{\tau(G)=\tau(G_1)\tau(G_2)}
\tag{7}
$$

In the example of two independent couplings, identify $b,c$ as $s$. The product of boundaries $(as) (sd)$ becomes the boundary $(asd)$. The highest grade remains $2$, but there are now three vertices and one connected component.

### The metric of the combined space

> [!info] Orthogonal gluing
> **Theorem.** After gluing at a single vertex, the metric within each part is preserved, and their spaces of internal vectors $W_1,W_2$ are orthogonal. Every vector of the combined space has a unique representation as $v_1+v_2$, where $v_i\in W_i$, and
>
> $$
> \|v_1+v_2\|^2=\|v_1\|^2+\|v_2\|^2
> \tag{8}
> $$

This is called an **orthogonal direct sum** of spaces and is written $W=W_1\oplus W_2$. The word “direct” refers to uniqueness of the decomposition; orthogonality means that inner products between the parts vanish.

> [!note]- Proof using polyforms
> **Proof.** The vectors from $s$ to the other vertices form a basis split into two disjoint sets. Thus $W$ is the direct sum of $W_1,W_2$.
>
> In the product $M_1M_2[v_1]^2$, a contribution to the spanning-tree form can arise only from the top grade of the second part. Therefore,
>
> $$
> u_M([v_1]^2)=u_1([v_1]^2)\tau(G_2)
> $$
>
> Dividing by (7) gives the original norm of $v_1$. The same holds for the second part.
>
> For the mixed form $[v_1,v_2]$, a nonzero top-grade contribution is impossible. Its left argument adds a direction from the first part, and its right argument adds one from the second. Each factor $M_i$, however, adds the same grade from its own part to both arguments. The two arguments of the combined spanning-tree form cannot both be filled this way. Consequently, $u_M([v_1,v_2])=0$ and $v_1\cdot v_2=0$. Polarization gives (8). $\square$

Write $R(x,y;G)$ for effective resistance in the specified graph. For a vertex $x$ in the first part and a vertex $y$ in the second, the vector $y-x$ decomposes through $s$. We obtain

$$
\boxed{R(x,y;G)=R(x,s;G_1)+R(s,y;G_2)}
\tag{9}
$$

For example, gluing two unit couplings produces a path with effective resistance $2$ between its endpoints. In resistance geometry, the two directions are perpendicular: the squared distance between the endpoints is $1+1$.

The mixed term remains in the identity of forms $[v_1+v_2]^2=[v_1]^2+\{v_1,v_2\}+[v_2]^2$. Orthogonality makes its metric evaluation zero, not the formal polyform itself.

## Resistance radius is additive

The vertices of a connected graph form a nondegenerate resistance simplex. Within its affine space, there is a unique sphere passing through all the vertices. Its center $h$ is called the circumcenter. It need not coincide with the centroid, the arithmetic mean of the vertices.

> [!info] Resistance radius
> **Definition.** The resistance radius of a graph is the squared radius of its circumsphere:
>
> $$
> \rho(G)=\|a-h\|^2=R_{\mathrm{sph}}^2
> $$
>
> Here $a$ is any basis vertex of the graph. The value is independent of this choice. Here “resistance” specifies a quadratic quantity: $\rho$ is the square of the linear radius $R_{\mathrm{sph}}$.

For a graph consisting of one coupling with effective resistance $r$, the center is the midpoint of a segment of length $\sqrt r$, so $\rho=r/4$. For a single vertex, we take $\rho=0$.

Here centers are calculated as elements of affine space: vertex combinations whose coefficients sum to $1$. We use the norms of their differences, not the norms of the centers themselves. In the extension with $z$, the affine combination giving a center need not be local; its local point is obtained by the correction $\pi(p)=p-p^2z/2$, as explained in the [[Nonlocal Elements, Spheres, and Their Centers#The center in the affine hull of the original vertices|note on nonlocal elements]].

> [!info] Resistance radius is additive
> **Theorem.** When two graphs are glued at a single vertex $s$, the circumcenter and resistance radius of the combined graph satisfy
>
> $$
> \boxed{h=h_1+h_2-s,\qquad \rho(G)=\rho(G_1)+\rho(G_2)}
> \tag{10}
> $$

> [!note]- Proof
> **Proof.** Put $v_i=h_i-s$. The vectors $v_1,v_2$ belong to the orthogonal spaces of the two parts. Since the common vertex lies on both original spheres, $v_i^2=\rho(G_i)$.
>
> The element $h=h_1+h_2-s$ has affine weight $1$. For a vertex $x$ of the first part,
>
> $$
> \|x-h\|^2=\|(x-s)-v_1\|^2+\|v_2\|^2
> =\rho(G_1)+\rho(G_2)
> $$
>
> The same argument applies to the second part. All vertices lie on the sphere with center $h$ and the stated squared radius. Uniqueness of the circumsphere completes the proof. $\square$

![Gluing and Resistance Radius](<images/Gluing and Resistance Radius.svg>)

*Figure 3. Gluing two unit couplings. In the resistance realization, their internal directions are orthogonal. The midpoints $h_1,h_2$ of the original segments give the circumcenter $h=h_1+h_2-s$ of the combined triangle; its resistance radius is $1/4+1/4=1/2$.*

Any tree can be assembled by successively gluing individual couplings at a single vertex. Thus (10) gives

$$
\rho(T)=\frac14\sum_{e\in E(T)}\frac1{c_e}
$$

Here $c_e$ is the coupling strength, and $1/c_e$ is its effective resistance in the tree. For example, the unit path and the unit star with three couplings have the same resistance radius $3/4$, although their maximum pairwise effective resistances differ: $3$ and $2$.

> [!info] Resistance radius is additive
> **Definition.** The resistance diameter is $D_{\mathrm{res}}=4\rho=(2R_{\mathrm{sph}})^2$. It is also additive under single-vertex gluing; for a tree, $D_{\mathrm{res}}(T)=\sum_e1/c_e$. The linear diameter is $2R_{\mathrm{sph}}$. The maximum pairwise effective resistance is a different parameter: for the star above it is $2$, whereas $D_{\mathrm{res}}=3$.

Different parameters obey different rules: resistance radii add, whereas the spanning-tree coefficients in (7) multiply. A broader comparison is given in [[Gluing and the Limits of Matroid Invariance|the note on matroid invariants]].

## When two vertices are shared

Gluing at two vertices can be performed in two steps. First identify one pair as $s$, obtaining an orthogonal sum. Then identify the second pair $a\in G_1$, $b\in G_2$. This time, an internal direction of the already combined space disappears.

Put $R_1=R(s,a;G_1)$ and $R_2=R(s,b;G_2)$. After the first step, (9) gives the effective resistance between $a,b$ as $R_1+R_2$. Applying (4), we obtain for the final graph

$$
\boxed{\tau(G)=\tau(G_1)\tau(G_2)(R_1+R_2)}
\tag{11}
$$

The couplings of the original parts are retained as separate contributions. If they have the same endpoints after identification, their coefficients add.

At the polyform level, the product $M_1M_2$ still applies after substituting the common vertices. The product of the top-grade boundaries of the parts, however, now vanishes: the boundaries have two common vertices. The spanning-tree form of the combined graph comes from the top grade of one part and the next-to-top grade of the other. This agrees with the two terms on the right of (11).

> [!example] Two paths form a cycle
> Take unit paths with two and three couplings. Identifying their corresponding endpoints produces a cycle with five couplings. For the paths, $\tau_1=\tau_2=1$, $R_1=2$, and $R_2=3$, so $\tau(G)=5$.
>
> Check the result independently: a spanning tree of the cycle is obtained by deleting one of its five couplings. There are exactly five such trees.

The second identification reduces the dimension and changes the metric within the parts according to (3). Thus preservation of internal effective resistances and additivity of resistance radius, proved for a single shared vertex, do not automatically extend to two shared vertices.

## Scope and continuation

For a connected graph with positive coupling strengths, the resistance metric on $W$ is positive definite. A nonzero disappearing direction therefore has $d^2>0$, and the orthogonal projection exists uniquely. Contracting all vertices leaves a single vertex: the space of internal vectors is zero-dimensional, $L=0$, $M=e$, and $\tau=1$.

In a disconnected graph, (3) and (4) cannot be applied to a pair of vertices from different components: there is no original finite $R_{ab}$ for that pair. The rule for gluing independent components must be used first. If some components remain disconnected, a common finite resistance metric still does not arise.

All results in this note are stated in spaces of affine vectors and elements with their resistance metric. The null vector is needed for the [[The Null Vector, Isotropic Representation, and the Boundary|isotropic representation]], but is not a prerequisite for these proofs. In a general indefinite metric, applying the projection formula requires a separate check of nondegeneracy.

Next, we return to a selected subspace without identifying vertices: we will consider how values prescribed at selected vertices extend to the whole graph, and how this relates to projections and the Dirichlet problem.

## Sources and status of the results

The algebra of forms, the boundary merging rules, and the metric identity follow the [[Core specification|published core specification]]. General forest polynomials and matroid parameters are not prerequisites for this note.

Vertex identification, multiplication of spanning-tree coefficients under gluing at a single vertex, and the resistance geometry of a graph are established constructions. Their presentation here starts from maps of objects and products of PMG polyforms. The formulas for changes in the metric, circumcenter, and resistance radius are proved in the text; no claim of novelty is made for these geometric laws. The expression of effective resistance as a ratio of spanning-tree coefficients is compared with [1], and the geometry of the resistance simplex and its circumsphere with [2].

The examples were calculated directly. Formulas (3), (4), (7), and (9)-(11) were also checked on small weighted graphs by direct calculation of reduced Laplacians and resistance Gram matrices; circumcenters were obtained by solving the equal-distance equations. The note contains no working hypotheses.
