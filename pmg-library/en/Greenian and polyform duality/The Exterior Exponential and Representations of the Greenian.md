[[ru/Гриниан и дуальность полиформ/Внешняя экспонента и представления гриниана|Русский]]

The grades of the Greenian exponential are generated by the same law as those of the Laplacian exponential: the ordinary exterior exponential of a grade-one form. For differences, use the notation $a_{ij}=a_j-a_i=(a_i a_j)$. In the [[The Canonical Vector Representation of the Greenian|main vector representation]], its terms are $\gamma_{ij}[a_{ij}]^2$, where $\gamma_{ij}=-\mathrm{Gr}_{ij}$. Their products are calculated using boundary merging and annihilation rules.

This note establishes the general algebraic mechanism: factorization, the appearance of $1/k!$, coefficients as minors, rank, exponential duality, and the centered transition to the Green matrix. Cut forms are treated as alternative representations and convenient verification examples. The general exponential identities require no graph; graph applications here are restricted to the symmetric case.

## The exponential of a grade-one form

Let $E$ be a real space of dimension $d\ge1$. Forms of grade $k$ belong to $\Lambda^kE\otimes\Lambda^kE$ and are written as linear combinations of symbols $[X,Y]$. We use the rules

$$
[X,Y][A,B]=[XA,YB],\qquad
[X]^2=[X,X],\qquad e=[1,1]
$$

Products in the arguments are exterior products. The product of forms itself is commutative: the interchange signs in the two arguments cancel. The definitions appear in [[Basic Objects and Operations of Polyform Algebra]].

Let $S$ be an arbitrary grade-one form. Then $S^{d+1}=0$, since $E$ has no exterior objects of grade $d+1$.

**Definition.** The exterior exponential of the form $S$ is the polyform

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal E(S)=\exp S=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\mathcal E_k(S),
\qquad
\mathcal E_k(S)=\frac{S^k}{k!},
\qquad
\mathcal E_0(S)=e
}
\tag{1}
$$

The series is finite, so no convergence issue arises. Its components are generated successively by

$$
\mathcal E_{k+1}(S)=\frac1{k+1}S\mathcal E_k(S)
$$

More generally,

$$
\mathcal E_j(S)\mathcal E_k(S)
=\binom{j+k}{j}\mathcal E_{j+k}(S)
\tag{2}
$$

For $j+k>d$, both sides vanish; set $\mathcal E_m=0$ for $m>d$. Equality (2) follows from $S^jS^k=S^{j+k}$ and the ratio of factorials.

These conditions link the grades. An arbitrary polyform with grade-zero term $e$ need not be the exponential of a grade-one form: the component $S$ may be chosen freely, but all subsequent components are then determined by it.

## How grades two and three arise

Start with three elementary quadratic forms

$$
f_1=\lambda_1[u_1]^2,\qquad
f_2=\lambda_2[u_2]^2,\qquad
f_3=\lambda_3[u_3]^2,\qquad
S=f_1+f_2+f_3
$$

For each of them,

$$
f_i^2=\lambda_i^2[u_i\wedge u_i]^2=0
$$

The square of the sum, however, is generally nonzero:

$$
S^2=2(f_1f_2+f_1f_3+f_2f_3)
$$

Each pair occurs in two orders. Therefore

$$
\mathcal E_2(S)
=\lambda_1\lambda_2[u_1u_2]^2
+\lambda_1\lambda_3[u_1u_3]^2
+\lambda_2\lambda_3[u_2u_3]^2
$$

In the cube, only products of three distinct factors survive. Their $3!$ orders give

$$
S^3=3!f_1f_2f_3,\qquad
\mathcal E_3(S)=\lambda_1\lambda_2\lambda_3[u_1u_2u_3]^2
$$

All powers above the third vanish, since every such product repeats at least one of the three forms. If $u_1,u_2,u_3$ are linearly dependent, the third grade already vanishes.

| Grade | Selected factors | Exponential component |
|---:|---|---|
| $0$ | None | $e$ |
| $1$ | One | $f_1+f_2+f_3$ |
| $2$ | Two distinct factors | $f_1f_2+f_1f_3+f_2f_3$ |
| $3$ | All three | $f_1f_2f_3$ |

Thus the factor $1/k!$ removes repeated counting of the order of the selected forms. It is not introduced separately to obtain duality or a graph interpretation.

## Factorization and an arbitrary number of terms

**Theorem.** For a representation of a symmetric form

$$
S=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\lambda_\ell[u_\ell]^2
$$

the following hold:

$$
\boxed{
\exp S=\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}\bigl(e+\lambda_\ell[u_\ell]^2\bigr)
}
\tag{3}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal E_k(S)=
\sum_{\ell_1<\cdots<\ell_k}
\lambda_{\ell_1}\cdots\lambda_{\ell_k}
[u_{\ell_1}\cdots u_{\ell_k}]^2
}
\tag{4}
$$

The coefficients $\lambda_\ell$ may be arbitrary real numbers.

**Proof.** For commuting forms $P,Q$ with no grade-zero term, the finite binomial formula gives

$$
\exp(P+Q)=\exp P\,\exp Q
$$

Since $(\lambda_\ell[u_\ell]^2)^2=0$, the exponential of an individual term is $e+\lambda_\ell[u_\ell]^2$. This proves (3). Expanding the product, grade $k$ selects $k$ distinct factors, giving (4). $\square$

The unit grade $\mathcal E_0=e$ comes from choosing $e$ in every factor. It is already part of the exponential.

If the selected $u_{\ell_1},\ldots,u_{\ell_k}$ are dependent, their term in (4) vanishes. With coefficients of both signs, terms may also cancel. Thus the number of factors alone does not determine the highest nonzero grade.

The analogous factorization holds for a nonsymmetric representation $S=\sum_\ell\lambda_\ell[u_\ell,v_\ell]$: each form $[u_\ell,v_\ell]$ has square zero. Formula (4) then contains the forms $[u_{\ell_1}\cdots u_{\ell_k},v_{\ell_1}\cdots v_{\ell_k}]$. The graph applications below are restricted to the symmetric case.

## Higher-grade coefficients are minors

Choose a basis $u_1,\ldots,u_d$ of $E$ and write

$$
S=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}s_{ij}[u_i,u_j],\qquad
\mathbf S=(s_{ij})
$$

For an increasing index set $I=(i_1<\cdots<i_k)$, put $u_I=u_{i_1}\cdots u_{i_k}$. Write $\mathbf S_{I,J}$ for the submatrix with the selected rows and columns.

**Theorem.** For every $0\le k\le d$,

$$
\boxed{
\frac{S^k}{k!}
=\sum_{\#I=\#J=k}\det(\mathbf S_{I,J})[u_I,u_J]
}
\tag{5}
$$

At $k=0$, the empty determinant is used and equals $1$. Formula (5) does not require $\mathbf S$ to be symmetric.

**Proof.** Expand $S^k$. Repeated indices in either exterior argument make a term vanish. For fixed index sets $I,J$, all permutations of their indices remain. Reordering the arguments increasingly introduces the signs of these permutations.

After the order of the first set is fixed, summing over permutations of the second gives the determinant $\det(\mathbf S_{I,J})$. The first set can be ordered in $k!$ ways; after signs are included, each gives the same determinant. Thus the coefficient in $S^k$ is $k!\det(\mathbf S_{I,J})$. Division by $k!$ gives (5). $\square$

For example, the grade-two coefficient of $[u_i u_j,u_p u_q]$, where $i<j$ and $p<q$, is

$$
s_{ip}s_{jq}-s_{iq}s_{jp}
$$

Grade three contains determinants of order $3$. Continuing to grade $d$ gives the determinant of the entire matrix.

This is the standard machinery of exterior powers and compound matrices [1]. In the formalism of double forms, the connection between the exterior exponential and the action of an operator on all exterior powers is established in [2, Proposition 5.1 of the open preprint].

## Rank and top grade

**Corollary.** If $\operatorname{rank}\mathbf S=r$, the highest nonzero grade of $\exp S$ is $r$:

$$
\mathcal E_r(S)\ne0,\qquad
\mathcal E_k(S)=0\quad(k>r)
\tag{6}
$$

For $S=0$, we have $r=0$ and $\exp S=e$.

**Proof.** Matrix rank is the largest order of a nonzero minor. Formula (5) translates this condition into a statement about grades. $\square$

Denote the fixed top form of the space by

$$
T_E=[u_1\cdots u_d]^2
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{\mathcal E_d(S)=(\det\mathbf S)T_E}
\tag{7}
$$

Thus the highest admissible grade $d$ must be distinguished from the highest nonzero grade of a particular exponential. They coincide if and only if $\mathbf S$ is nonsingular.

The determinant in (7) refers to the chosen basis and corresponding form $T_E$. A change of basis changes both factors, while the component $\mathcal E_d(S)$ itself remains the same object.

Nilpotence of $S$ in the polyform algebra is compatible with nonsingularity of its coefficient matrix. For example, when $\mathbf S=\mathbf I$, all matrix eigenvalues equal $1$, but $S^{d+1}=0$. Thus $\exp S$ and the ordinary matrix exponential $\exp\mathbf S$ have different meanings.

## Canonical vector factors

Let $E$ be generated by $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ with the sole relation $\sum_i a_i=0$. As proved in the [[The Canonical Vector Representation of the Greenian#Why the vector representation is canonical|vector basis theorem]], every symmetric grade-one form has a unique expression

$$
S=\sum_{i<j}\gamma_{ij}[a_{ij}]^2
$$

Formulas (3)–(4) immediately give

$$
\exp S=\prod_{i<j}\bigl(e+\gamma_{ij}[a_{ij}]^2\bigr)
$$

Here a repeated edge or a cycle makes the product vanish, while a tree merges into the boundary of its component. Real weights $\gamma_{ij}$ give signed forest sums. This is the main calculation method for Laplacian and Greenian exponentials. Their first grades are vector forms, while higher grades are forms on exterior products of vectors, that is, boundary forms. A detailed calculation of grades two and three for a path appears in the [[The Canonical Vector Representation of the Greenian#Full example: a path on four vertices|vector multiplication example]].

The cut expansion below is used to compare equal forms and check the exponential through a different set of factors.

## Cut forms without an underlying graph

Now take the space of dual elements generated by $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ with the sole relation

$$
a_1+\cdots+a_n=0,\qquad \dim E=n-1
$$

This can be understood as the quotient space of coordinate functionals defined in the [[Laplacian and Greenian - Mutual Duality of Exponentials#Why the dual vertices sum to zero|construction of dual elements]]. No graph is needed for this space.

For a nonempty proper subset $A\subset\mathcal V=\{a_1,\ldots,a_n\}$, set

$$
a_A=\sum_{a_i\in A}a_i
$$

The partition $A\mid A^c$ corresponds to the cut form $[a_A]^2$. Since $a_{A^c}=-a_A$, both parts give the same form.

**Definition.** A grade-one cut polyform is a specified sum

$$
S=\sum_{A\mid A^c}\mu_A[a_A]^2
$$

Each unordered partition is counted once. Here the coefficients $\mu_A$ are arbitrary real numbers.

By (3)–(4),

$$
\boxed{
\exp S=\prod_{A\mid A^c}\bigl(e+\mu_A[a_A]^2\bigr)
}
\tag{8}
$$

and its grade $k$ is obtained by selecting $k$ distinct cut forms. Exterior dependencies determine which products vanish; the others combine according to the algebraic rules.

Cut forms span the entire space of symmetric grade-one forms. Indeed, $a_1,\ldots,a_{n-1}$ may be chosen as a basis, and polar forms can be expressed through quadratic forms:

$$
\{a_i,a_j\}=[a_i+a_j]^2-[a_i]^2-[a_j]^2
$$

For $n=2$, the single form $[a_1]^2$ suffices. Representing an arbitrary symmetric form may require coefficients of both signs.

A cut expansion need not be unique. If two sums define the same $S$, their exponentials coincide, although their factor lists may differ. A specific identity of this kind is examined in the [[Cuts and Forest Grades of the Greenian Exponential#Uniqueness of the form and nonuniqueness of its expansion|comparison of the forest and reduced representations]].

## Explicit generation of grades on four elements

Let $a+b+c+d=0$ in a dual space of dimension $3$. Consider the form with arbitrary real coefficients

$$
S=\lambda_1[a]^2+\lambda_2[a+b]^2+\lambda_3[d]^2
$$

Its arguments $a$, $a+b$, and $d$ are independent. No graph interpretation is specified yet.

The factor representation is

$$
\exp S=
(e+\lambda_1[a]^2)
(e+\lambda_2[a+b]^2)
(e+\lambda_3[d]^2)
$$

The first grade is $S$. In grade two, we obtain

$$
\mathcal E_2(S)=
\lambda_1\lambda_2[a(a+b)]^2+
\lambda_1\lambda_3[ad]^2+
\lambda_2\lambda_3[(a+b)d]^2
$$

In the exterior algebra,

$$
a(a+b)=ab,\qquad
(a+b)d=(-c-d)d=-cd
$$

Thus all components of the exponential are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal E_0&=e\\
\mathcal E_1&=\lambda_1[a]^2+\lambda_2[a+b]^2+\lambda_3[d]^2\\
\mathcal E_2&=\lambda_1\lambda_2[ab]^2+\lambda_1\lambda_3[ad]^2+\lambda_2\lambda_3[cd]^2\\
\mathcal E_3&=\lambda_1\lambda_2\lambda_3[abd]^2
\end{aligned}
\tag{9}
$$

Here $\mathcal E_k=S^k/k!$. For nonzero $\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3$, the top grade $3$ is reached. Setting one coefficient to zero makes the rank $2$ and removes grade three.

For example, for $(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)=(2,3,5)$, the grade-two coefficients are $6,10,15$, and the top coefficient is $30$.

### Three factors but only two nonzero positive grades

In the same space, take

$$
S_0=[a]^2+[b]^2+[a+b]^2
$$

The three arguments lie in a two-dimensional subspace. All three pairwise products give the same quadratic form:

$$
[ab]^2=[a(a+b)]^2=[b(a+b)]^2
$$

Consequently,

$$
\exp S_0=e+S_0+3[ab]^2,\qquad S_0^3=0
$$

This example shows both the addition of identical contributions and the termination of grade generation caused by dependent arguments.

## The dual exponential of a nondegenerate symmetric form

No inverse form has been needed so far. To connect with duality, now assume that the coefficient matrix is nonsingular and symmetric.

Let $u_1,\ldots,u_d$ be a basis of the direct space, $u^1,\ldots,u^d$ its dual basis, and

$$
P=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}[u_i,u_j],\qquad
\mathbf A^{\mathsf T}=\mathbf A,\qquad
\Delta=\det\mathbf A\ne0
$$

Fix

$$
\Omega=u_1\cdots u_d,\qquad T=[\Omega]^2
$$

In the dual space, define the form

$$
S=\sum_{i,j}(\mathbf A^{-1})_{ij}[u^i,u^j]
$$

Deletion from direct objects means the interior product with dual arguments.

**Theorem (exponential duality).** For $0\le k\le d$,

$$
\boxed{
(\Delta T)/\left(\frac{S^k}{k!}\right)
=\frac{P^{d-k}}{(d-k)!}
}
\tag{10}
$$

Consequently, the polyform dual to $\exp P$ relative to its top component $\Delta T$ is

$$
\boxed{D=\exp S,\qquad (\Delta T)/D=\exp P}
\tag{11}
$$

**Proof.** The real symmetric matrix $\mathbf A$ can be diagonalized by an orthogonal coordinate change. In matched direct and dual bases $v_i,v^i$, we obtain

$$
P=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_i[v_i]^2,\qquad
S=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_i^{-1}[v^i]^2,\qquad
\Delta=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_i
$$

All $\lambda_i$ are nonzero, but may have either sign. The orthogonal change has determinant $\pm1$, so $T=[v_1\cdots v_d]^2$.

By (4), the term of $S^k/k!$ indexed by a $k$-element set $I$ is

$$
\left(\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_i^{-1}\right)[v^I]^2
$$

Upon deletion from $\Delta T$, this term's coefficient is multiplied by $\Delta$ and becomes

$$
\Delta\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_i^{-1}
=\prod_{j\notin I}\lambda_j
$$

Deleting $v^I$ from the top exterior product leaves the complementary set $v_{I^c}$ up to sign. The sign disappears in the quadratic form. Thus the action on the left-hand side of (10) gives the sum

$$
\sum_{\#I=k}
\left(\prod_{j\notin I}\lambda_j\right)[v_{I^c}]^2
=\frac{P^{d-k}}{(d-k)!}
$$

This proves (10), and summing over $k$ gives (11). $\square$

Orthogonality in this proof refers to auxiliary coordinates in the chosen basis. No PMG metric inner product is introduced here.

The theorem explains the general mechanism: complementary grades of the exponential pass to exterior powers of the inverse form, while the determinant enters the top component of the direct exponential used as the reference for deletion. When $\mathbf A$ is singular, the exponential $\exp P$ exists, but the formula using $\mathbf A^{-1}$ does not apply.

Apply the same theorem to $S$ and the matrix $\mathbf A^{-1}$. Its inverse is $\mathbf A$, and the dual of the dual space is naturally identified with the original space. If $M_j=P^j/j!$ and $Q_k=S^k/k!$, then

$$
M_d/Q_k=M_{d-k},\qquad
Q_d/M_k=Q_{d-k}
$$

Thus exponential duality is mutual. The top components of the two exponentials serve as references for deletion in opposite directions.

Matrix inversion in the definition of $S$ must be distinguished from inversion of the exponential under polyform multiplication. The latter is always given by $\exp P\,\exp(-P)=e$ and does not require $\mathbf A$ to be nonsingular.

## Application to the metric polyform of a graph

Consider a connected undirected graph on $n\ge2$ vertices with positive conductances. Let

$$
M=\exp L,\qquad
T=[(a_1\ldots a_n)]^2,\qquad
d=n-1
$$

Choose the direct basis

$$
u_i=a_i-a_n,\qquad 1\le i<n
$$

Its dual basis consists of the dual elements $a_1,\ldots,a_{n-1}$: $u_j/a_i=\delta_{ij}$. Also, $[u_1\cdots u_d]^2=T$.

In this basis, the coefficient matrix of $L$ is the reduced Laplacian

$$
\mathbf A=\mathbf L_{\widehat n,\widehat n},\qquad
L=\sum_{i,j<n}A_{ij}[u_i,u_j],\qquad
\det\mathbf A=\tau
$$

Equality of the forms follows from the zero row and column sums of $\mathbf L$; equality of the determinants follows from Kirchhoff's theorem.

Applying (10), we obtain for the form on dual arguments

$$
S=\sum_{i,j<n}(\mathbf A^{-1})_{ij}[a_i,a_j]
$$

the equality $(\tau T)/S=M_{d-1}$. By the definition and uniqueness of the dual grade, $S=Q_1$.

**Corollary (the exponential law for dual grades).**

$$
\boxed{
Q_k=\frac{Q_1^k}{k!},
\qquad
D=\exp Q_1,\qquad Q_0=e
}
\tag{12}
$$

For the auxiliary forest normalization $\widetilde Q_k=\tau Q_k$, $\widetilde D=\tau D$,

$$
\widetilde Q_0=\tau e,\qquad
\widetilde Q_k=\frac{\widetilde Q_1^k}{k!\tau^{k-1}}\quad(1\le k\le d)
\tag{13}
$$

Thus the entire series is determined by the first dual form. Its unit and all its coefficients arise from one ordinary exponential.

In the [[Cuts and Forest Grades of the Greenian Exponential#Representing the Greenian through cuts|forest representation of the cut form]],

$$
Q_1=\sum_{A\mid A^c}\widehat\mu_A[a_A]^2,\qquad
\widehat\mu_A=\frac{\tau(G[A])\tau(G[A^c])}{\tau(G)}
$$

Thus factorization (8) becomes a factorization of the Greenian exponential in dual notation. Comparing (12) with the [[Cuts and Forest Grades of the Greenian Exponential#The forest formula for grades of the Greenian exponential|forest formula for higher grades]] gives

$$
\frac{Q_1^k}{k!}
=\frac1\tau
\sum_{F\in\mathcal F_{k+1}(G)}w(F)\Phi_{\pi(F)}
\tag{14}
$$

These are equalities of full sums of forms. An individual product of $k$ cut forms need not correspond to one spanning $(k+1)$-forest.

## The centered image and the Greenian

So far, $S=Q_1$ is a form on dual elements. To compare it with the Greenian on direct vectors, we must specify the identification of the spaces. Let $W$ denote the space of vertex differences and $W^*$ its dual space.

Let

$$
h=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i
$$

be the centroid of the direct vertices; set $p_i=a_i-h$. In this section, classes of coordinate functionals are written explicitly as $a_i^\vee$ to distinguish them from direct vertices $a_i$.

**Definition.** The centered identification is the linear map

$$
\mathcal C:W^*\longrightarrow W,\qquad
a_i^\vee\longmapsto p_i=a_i-h
\tag{15}
$$

The relation $\sum_i a_i^\vee=0$ maps to $\sum_i p_i=0$. The vectors $p_1,\ldots,p_{n-1}$ are independent, so $\mathcal C$ is an isomorphism. In the shorter dual notation above, the same classes were denoted by $a_i$.

Extend this map to exterior objects factor by factor and to forms in both arguments. Then $\mathcal C$ preserves products, and

$$
\mathcal C(\exp S)=\exp(\mathcal C(S))
$$

This identification is determined by the original vertex basis and subtraction of the mean. It differs from the metric identification of vectors and covectors through the resistance inner product.

Centering preserves any form on differences, including one specified independently of a Laplacian. The zero-sum relation concerns centered or dual elements, not independent direct vertices. Preservation of boundary identities and their cut images are examined in [[Symmetric Identities of the Greenian]].

**Theorem.** The coefficient matrix of $\mathcal C(Q_1)$ in the direct vertices is the Green matrix $\mathbf{Gr}=\mathbf L^+$.

**Proof.** Let $\mathbf U$ contain the coordinate columns of $a_i-a_n$, $i<n$, and let $\mathbf C$ contain the columns of $a_i-h$, $i<n$, in the original vertex basis. Directly from these columns,

$$
\mathbf U^{\mathsf T}\mathbf C=\mathbf I_d,\qquad
\mathbf U\mathbf C^{\mathsf T}=\mathbf J,\qquad
\mathbf J=\mathbf I_n-\frac1n\mathbf1\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}
$$

Also, $\mathbf L=\mathbf U\mathbf A\mathbf U^{\mathsf T}$. The coefficient matrix of the centered image is

$$
\mathbf K=\mathbf C\mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf C^{\mathsf T}
$$

It is symmetric, satisfies $\mathbf K\mathbf1=0$, and

$$
\mathbf L\mathbf K
=\mathbf U\mathbf A(\mathbf U^{\mathsf T}\mathbf C)
\mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf C^{\mathsf T}
=\mathbf U\mathbf C^{\mathsf T}
=\mathbf J
$$

By symmetry, also $\mathbf K\mathbf L=\mathbf J$. Thus $\mathbf K$ inverts $\mathbf L$ on the space of columns with zero sum and vanishes on constant columns. These are the defining properties of the Green matrix, so $\mathbf K=\mathbf{Gr}$. $\square$

**Definition.** The Greenian is the grade-one form

$$
\mathrm{Gr}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathrm{Gr}_{ij}[a_i-h,a_j-h]
$$

Because the row and column sums vanish, the same form can be written as $\mathrm{Gr}=\sum_{i,j}\mathrm{Gr}_{ij}[a_i,a_j]$.

Thus

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal C(Q_1)=\mathrm{Gr},
\qquad
\mathcal C(D)=\exp(\mathrm{Gr})
}
\tag{16}
$$

The Greenian and $Q_1$ are the centered and dual representations of one construction under identification (15). “Greenian” names the form; “duality” describes the relationship between its exponential and the Laplacian exponential. Both exponentials use polyform multiplication, not matrix multiplication.

### Representation through resistances

The [[Laplacian, Green Matrix, and Effective Resistance Matrix#Reconstructing the Green matrix|double-centering formula]] establishes

$$
\mathbf{Gr}=-\frac12\mathbf J\mathbf R\mathbf J
$$

where $\mathbf R=(R_{ij})$ is the resistance matrix. Transferring this equality through (15) gives, on dual arguments,

$$
\boxed{
Q_1=-\frac12\sum_{i<j}R_{ij}\{a_i^\vee,a_j^\vee\}
}
\tag{17}
$$

Indeed, the centered image of the right-hand side has matrix $-\mathbf J\mathbf R\mathbf J/2$: the diagonal of $\mathbf R$ is zero, and the polar form $\{a_i^\vee,a_j^\vee\}$ accounts for both argument orders. Injectivity of $\mathcal C$ completes the comparison.

Setting $a_{ij}^\vee=a_j^\vee-a_i^\vee$ gives the vector expression for the first dual form:

$$
Q_1=-\sum_{i<j}\mathrm{Gr}_{ij}[a_{ij}^\vee]^2
$$

It follows from the zero row sums of $\mathbf{Gr}$ upon expanding squared differences. The canonical nature of the expression and the interpretation of $-\mathrm{Gr}_{ij}$ through centroid products are proved in [[The Canonical Vector Representation of the Greenian]]. The polar, cut, and centered expressions give the same form in the corresponding spaces. Their coefficients have different interpretations.

## Products of exponentials and matrix composition

After centered identification, the Laplacian and the Greenian belong to the same algebra of forms on $W$. Its product is commutative, so

$$
\boxed{
\exp L\,\exp(\mathrm{Gr})=\exp(L+\mathrm{Gr})
}
$$

This is a boundary polyform built from the sum of two vector forms. Its first grade is $L+\mathrm{Gr}$, which already distinguishes it from the unit and from the exponential of the form with matrix $\mathbf J$.

**Matrix composition** gives a different equality:

$$
\mathbf L\mathbf{Gr}=\mathbf{Gr}\mathbf L=\mathbf J
$$

The projector $\mathbf J$ corresponds to the vector form of the complete graph with weights $1/n$. This form arises from matrix composition, not the exterior product of exponentials. The inverse of $\exp L$ under polyform multiplication is $\exp(-L)$.

Finally, deletion lowers grade and relates the exponentials through $M_d/D=M$ and $Q_d/M=D$. Before identifying the spaces, “the product of $M$ and $D$” requires an operation to be specified: deletion, transfer to one space, or action of the corresponding operators.

## Two graph checks

### A path on four vertices

For the path $a-b-c-d$ with positive conductances $c_{ab},c_{bc},c_{cd}$, we have $\tau=c_{ab}c_{bc}c_{cd}$. The forest formula gives

$$
Q_1=
\frac{[a]^2}{c_{ab}}+
\frac{[a+b]^2}{c_{bc}}+
\frac{[d]^2}{c_{cd}}
$$

This is algebraic example (9) with $\lambda_1=1/c_{ab}$, $\lambda_2=1/c_{bc}$, and $\lambda_3=1/c_{cd}$. Its highest grade is $[abd]^2/\tau$, as required by the definition of duality.

The centered image is

$$
\mathrm{Gr}=
\frac{[a-h]^2}{c_{ab}}+
\frac{[a+b-2h]^2}{c_{bc}}+
\frac{[d-h]^2}{c_{cd}}
$$

For unit conductances, its coefficient matrix is

$$
\mathbf{Gr}=\frac18
\begin{pmatrix}
7&1&-3&-5\\
1&3&-1&-3\\
-3&-1&3&1\\
-5&-3&1&7
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Direct multiplication by the path Laplacian matrix gives $\mathbf L\mathbf{Gr}=\mathbf J$, and the row sums of $\mathbf{Gr}$ vanish. This independently checks the centered representation.

### Normalization on K4

For the unit complete graph on four vertices, $\tau=16$, and the [[Cuts and Forest Grades of the Greenian Exponential#Uniqueness of the form and nonuniqueness of its expansion|reduced expression for the first form]] gives

$$
\widetilde Q_1=4([a]^2+[b]^2+[c]^2+[d]^2),\qquad
Q_1=\frac14([a]^2+[b]^2+[c]^2+[d]^2)
$$

Ordinary expansion of powers gives

$$
\frac{Q_1^2}{2!}
=\frac1{16}\bigl([ab]^2+[ac]^2+[ad]^2+[bc]^2+[bd]^2+[cd]^2\bigr)
$$

In grade three, the four products of three distinct vertices coincide up to sign, so

$$
\frac{Q_1^3}{3!}=\frac1{16}[abc]^2
$$

These are exactly $Q_2$ and $Q_3$ from the forest calculation. The centered image of the first grade has matrix $\mathbf{Gr}=\mathbf J/4$, the inverse of $\mathbf L=4\mathbf J$ on $\mathbf1^\perp$.

## Scope of results and further applications

The exponential, factorization, minor, and rank formulas are algebraic. They apply without a graph; the exponential requires neither positivity nor invertibility of the form. Theorem (10) on the inverse form assumes symmetry and nondegeneracy. Graph conclusions (12)–(17) are proved here for connected undirected graphs with positive conductances.

Independent calculations were also performed in exact rational arithmetic. On 29 matrices of orders 1–5, including nonsymmetric, singular, and symmetric indefinite matrices, direct exterior powers were checked against all minors of the corresponding order. For 15 nonsingular symmetric matrices, deletion was checked in every grade using (10). The factorization and both algebraic examples on four elements were checked by expanding products. For the unit path, the path with conductances $2,3,5$, and $K_4$, all normalized forest grades obtained by enumeration agreed with powers of the inverse form; the centered image was independently checked against matrix pseudoinversion. These finite checks supplement the general proofs.

Exterior algebra and its application to determinants are presented in [1]. The formalism of double forms, the interior product, and exterior extensions of operators are discussed in [2, §§2, 4–5 of the open preprint]. Here this machinery is applied in PMG notation; the proofs specify the normalization of the top form explicitly.

Higher grades of the exponential contain no independently selectable parameters beyond the first grade. For a graph form $Q_1$, centering recovers $\mathbf{Gr}$, then $\mathbf L=\mathbf{Gr}^+$ and the graph conductances. Thus the first dual form also determines $\tau$ and the entire Greenian exponential.

The main vector expression, signed forests, and the normalization $1/(n^2\tau)$ at the full dual boundary are examined in [[The Canonical Vector Representation of the Greenian]]. The transition to resistances and original forest weights for vertex addition and deletion is presented in [[Greenian Coefficients and Vertex Variations]]. Products of direct and dual exponentials also require the operation to be specified: the exterior product of forms, deletion, or composition of the corresponding operators.

The checks are reproduced by the [exact-arithmetic script](https://github.com/magin67/pmg-notes/blob/v5/checks/dual/verify_exponential.py). From the repository root:
