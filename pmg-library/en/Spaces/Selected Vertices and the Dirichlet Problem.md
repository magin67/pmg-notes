Select several vertices of a graph and assign numerical values to them. How can we determine values at the remaining vertices while taking the coupling coefficients into account? For example, scores may be known for several objects, and we want a consistent extension to the others: differences between strongly coupled objects should have a greater effect on the result.

We will consider the Dirichlet problem and establish its geometric meaning. The coefficients with which the prescribed values enter the solution will turn out to be the bi-coordinates of orthogonal projections onto the subspace generated by the selected vertices. We will compute these coefficients using a metric polyform.

It uses [[Spaces, Subspaces, and the Choice of a Basis|affine subspaces]], [[Projections, Bi-coordinates, and Di-coordinates|coordinates and projections]], [[Vertex Contraction and Graph Gluing|the metric after contraction]], and the [[Core specification|metric identity]]. The main graph is finite, connected, and undirected, with positive coefficients on all existing couplings. We will consider the disconnected case separately.

## Prescribed values and internal vertices

Write $\mathcal V$ for the set of all vertices, $P$ for the nonempty selected set, and $I=\mathcal V\setminus P$ for the remaining vertices. We call the vertices in $I$ internal. For now, assume that there is at least one internal vertex.

A function $f$ assigns to each vertex $a_i$ a real number $f_i=f(a_i)$. Its values on $P$ are prescribed. Coupling coefficients are denoted by $c_{ij}=c_{ji}\ge0$, with $c_{ij}=0$ when no coupling exists. There are no loops, so $c_{ii}=0$.

> [!info] The Dirichlet problem on a graph
> **Definition.** Extend the prescribed values on $P$ so that every internal vertex satisfies
>
> $$
> \sum_{j\in\mathcal V}c_{xj}(f_x-f_j)=0,\qquad x\in I
> \tag{1}
> $$
>
> Here the index $x$ denotes an internal vertex. The sum includes all its neighbors, including selected vertices.

If $d_x=\sum_jc_{xj}$ is the weighted degree of the vertex, (1) can be written as

$$
f_x=\frac{\sum_jc_{xj}f_j}{d_x}
$$

The value at an internal vertex is the weighted average of its neighbors' values. A function with this property is called **harmonic at the internal vertices**, and the solution is called a harmonic extension. This is the standard name for a property of the function [1]; it does not introduce a new name for bi-coordinates.

In the literature, $P$ is usually called the boundary of the Dirichlet problem. In PMG, “boundary” already denotes the result of applying $\partial$, as in $(ab)=b-a$. We therefore use “selected vertices” below.

> [!example] A path with unequal coupling coefficients
> Consider the path $a-x-y-b$, where $c_{ax}=2$, $c_{xy}=1$, and $c_{yb}=3$. Select $P=\{a,b\}$ and prescribe $f_a=0$, $f_b=11$. At the internal vertices we obtain
>
> $$
> 2(f_x-0)+(f_x-f_y)=0,\qquad
> (f_y-f_x)+3(f_y-11)=0
> $$
>
> Hence $3f_x-f_y=0$, $-f_x+4f_y=33$, and therefore $f_x=3$, $f_y=9$.

## Minimum energy and uniqueness

Conditions (1) arise from minimizing the **Dirichlet energy**

$$
E(f)=\sum_{i<j}c_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2
\tag{2}
$$

Each undirected coupling is counted once. A larger coefficient gives a greater contribution for the same difference in values. With $f_p$, $p\in P$, fixed, only the internal values vary.

The connection with the Laplacian polyform is already visible here:

$$
L=\sum_{i<j}c_{ij}[(a_i a_j)]^2
$$

Extend $f$ linearly to the formal vertex space: $f(\sum_i t_i a_i)=\sum_i t_if_i$. Substituting $[u,v]\mapsto f(u)f(v)$ into the grade-1 form $L$ gives exactly (2), since $f((a_i a_j))=f_j-f_i$. This evaluates the form using function values and differs from the metric evaluation $[u,v]\mapsto u\cdot v$. The potential of a form, $u_M([u,v])$, is also distinct from a function value at a vertex.

**Theorem.** For a connected graph and nonempty $P$, the Dirichlet problem has a unique solution. It is the unique minimizer of the energy (2) subject to the prescribed values on $P$.

> [!proof]- Proof
> Let $h$ vanish on $P$ and represent a change in the internal values. Expanding the squares and collecting the terms for each $h_x$ gives
>
> $$
> E(f+h)=E(f)+2\sum_{x\in I}h_x\sum_jc_{xj}(f_x-f_j)+E(h)
> $$
>
> If $E(h)=0$, the values of $h$ agree at the endpoints of every existing coupling. Connectedness makes these values constant throughout the graph, and on $P$ they are zero. Thus $h=0$.
>
> The quadratic part of the energy in the internal variables is therefore positive definite. Such a quadratic function has a unique minimum. At this minimum, the coefficient of the linear change in each variable vanishes, giving (1). Conversely, when (1) holds, we have $E(f+h)=E(f)+E(h)$, and every nonzero admissible change increases the energy. $\square$

For the path example, the energy is $2f_x^2+(f_x-f_y)^2+3(f_y-11)^2$. Its value at the solution is $66$. Setting $f_x=3+\alpha$, $f_y=9+\beta$ gives directly

$$
E(f)=66+2\alpha^2+(\alpha-\beta)^2+3\beta^2
$$

The minimum is attained only at $\alpha=\beta=0$.

In the electrical interpretation, the $c_{ij}$ are conductances, the $f_i$ are vertex potentials, and (1) expresses the absence of external current injections at internal vertices. This interpretation is not required to formulate the problem itself.

## The subspace generated by the selected vertices

The selected vertices determine the affine subspace

$$
\mathcal A_P=\left\{\sum_{p\in P}t_p p:\sum_{p\in P}t_p=1\right\}
$$

Its direction space consists of combinations of the same vertices with zero coefficient sum:

$$
W_P=\left\{\sum_{p\in P}t_p p:\sum_{p\in P}t_p=0\right\}
$$

The metric on $W_P$ is the restriction of the graph's original resistance metric. Selecting a subspace does not delete vertices or recalculate couplings.

For an internal vertex $x$, let $q_x$ denote its orthogonal projection onto $\mathcal A_P$, and let $r_x=x-q_x$ be the residual vector. The projection conditions are

$$
q_x\in\mathcal A_P,\qquad r_x\cdot h=0\quad\text{for all }h\in W_P
$$

The selected vertices are part of the original independent basis. The frame of the projection in these vertices is therefore unique:

$$
\boxed{q_x=\sum_{p\in P}b_{xp}p,\qquad \sum_{p\in P}b_{xp}=1}
\tag{3}
$$

The numbers $b_{xp}$ are the bi-coordinates of $q_x$ in the basis of selected vertices. This is a special case of the general definition of bi-coordinates: a point is expanded in points. The original vertex $x\notin P$ itself has no expansion in $P$.

Choose a reference vertex $p_0\in P$. The vectors $p-p_0$, with $p\ne p_0$, form a basis of $W_P$. Relative to this basis, the differences $x-p_0$ and $q_x-p_0$ have the same di-coordinates, that is, the same inner products:

$$
(x-p_0)\cdot(p-p_0)=(q_x-p_0)\cdot(p-p_0)
$$

This determines the projection by the rules in the [[Projections, Bi-coordinates, and Di-coordinates#Which coordinates determine the projection|note on coordinates]]. For a graph, the resulting coefficients have an additional meaning.

## Why the same coefficients extend values

> [!info] Projections and the influence matrix
> **Theorem.** If $b_{xp}$ are defined by the geometric expansion (3), then the solution of the Dirichlet problem for any prescribed values on $P$ is
>
> $$
> \boxed{f_x=\sum_{p\in P}b_{xp}f_p,\qquad x\in I}
> \tag{4}
> $$
>
> The matrix $\mathbf B=(b_{xp})$, whose rows correspond to internal vertices and whose columns correspond to selected vertices, is called the **influence matrix**.

Thus the geometric expansion of a projection and the extension of numerical values use the same coefficients. The value $f_x$ is obtained by evaluating the affine function on $\mathcal A_P$ at $q_x$, with the prescribed values $f_p$ at the selected vertices.

For the proof, introduce a vector formed from the couplings of an internal vertex:

$$
s_x=\sum_jc_{xj}(x-a_j)
$$

**Lemma.** For every affine vector $h=\sum_i h_i a_i$ with $\sum_i h_i=0$, one has $s_x\cdot h=h_x$. In particular, $s_x$ is orthogonal to $W_P$.

> [!proof]- Proof of the lemma and theorem
> For a short verification of the lemma, use the matrix representation from the specification. Let $\mathbf L$ be the Laplacian matrix, $\mathbf{Gr}$ the Green matrix, and $\mathbf e_x$ the column with a unit entry in position $x$ and zeros elsewhere. The coordinate column of $s_x$ is $\mathbf L\mathbf e_x$. For a column $\mathbf h$ with zero sum, the PMG core gives $\mathbf L\mathbf{Gr}\mathbf h=\mathbf h$. Hence
>
> $$
> s_x\cdot h=\mathbf e_x^{\mathsf T}\mathbf L\mathbf{Gr}\mathbf h=h_x
> $$
>
> If $h\in W_P$, its internal coordinate $h_x$ is zero. This proves the lemma.
>
> For a selected vertex, set $q_p=p$. Orthogonally project $s_x$ onto $W_P$. Its projection is zero, and the projection of the difference $x-a_j$ is $q_x-q_j$. Therefore,
>
> $$
> \sum_jc_{xj}(q_x-q_j)=0
> $$
>
> Substitute the frames of the $q_j$ into this equality. Independence of the selected vertices means that the coefficient of each must vanish:
>
> $$
> \sum_jc_{xj}(b_{xp}-b_{jp})=0
> $$
>
> Here, for $j\in P$, the coefficient $b_{jp}$ is $1$ when $j=p$ and $0$ otherwise. For each fixed $p$, the coefficients $b_{jp}$ therefore solve the Dirichlet problem with value one at $p$ and zero at the other selected vertices. Taking a linear combination of these solutions with coefficients $f_p$ gives (4). Uniqueness has already been proved. $\square$

Each column of $\mathbf B$ describes the influence of one selected vertex: prescribe $1$ there and $0$ at the other selected vertices, then calculate the internal values.

### A geometric calculation on the path

In our example, set $u=x-a$, $v=y-x$, and $w=b-y$. For a tree, the vectors of distinct couplings are orthogonal in the resistance metric, and their norms are the reciprocals of the coupling coefficients. Here,

$$
u^2=\frac12,\qquad v^2=1,\qquad w^2=\frac13,\qquad
u\cdot v=u\cdot w=v\cdot w=0
$$

These equalities can also be checked using the path's effective resistances and the inner-product formula. The direction of the selected line is $d=b-a=u+v+w$, so $d^2=11/6$. The projection coefficients along this direction are

$$
\frac{(x-a)\cdot d}{d^2}=\frac{1/2}{11/6}=\frac3{11},\qquad
\frac{(y-a)\cdot d}{d^2}=\frac{3/2}{11/6}=\frac9{11}
$$

Consequently,

$$
q_x=\frac{8a+3b}{11},\qquad q_y=\frac{2a+9b}{11},\qquad
\mathbf B=\frac1{11}\begin{pmatrix}8&3\\2&9\end{pmatrix}
$$

The matrix rows are ordered as $x,y$ and the columns as $a,b$. With $f_a=0$, $f_b=11$, formula (4) again gives $3$ and $9$.

## Contraction and the metric of residuals

We now compute the coefficients using polyforms. Identify all vertices in $P$ as a single vertex $o$, retaining the internal vertices. Recall the [[Vertex Contraction and Graph Gluing#Which subspace does contraction produce?|result on contraction]]: the metric of the new graph is realized on the orthogonal complement of the directions that disappear. Here $W_P$ disappears, and its orthogonal complement consists of all vectors perpendicular to $W_P$.

For $x\in I$, the vector $v_x=(ox)=x-o$ of the contracted graph corresponds to the residual $r_x=x-q_x$ in the original geometry. Indeed, for any $p_0\in P$, the vector $x-p_0$ splits into $q_x-p_0\in W_P$ and $r_x\perp W_P$.

The vectors $r_x$ are independent: in the original vertex basis, the internal coordinate of $r_x$ is $1$ in position $x$ and $0$ in the other internal positions. There are $|I|$ such vectors, equal to the dimension of the orthogonal complement. They therefore form a basis of that complement.

Contraction and projection onto $\mathcal A_P$ describe two different parts of the original geometry. Projection retains the component along the selected subspace, while the metric of the contracted graph describes the residuals relative to it.

### The fundamental matrix from potentials

Let $d_x^P=\sum_{p\in P}c_{xp}$ be the sum of coupling coefficients from an internal vertex to the selected vertices. The Laplacian and metric polyform of the contracted graph are

$$
\widehat L=\sum_{\substack{\{x,y\}\subset I}}c_{xy}[v_y-v_x]^2+\sum_{x\in I}d_x^P[v_x]^2,\qquad
\widehat M=\exp\widehat L
$$

The first sum runs over unordered pairs of internal vertices. Couplings within $P$ disappear, while all couplings from $x$ to $P$ combine into a single coupling $xo$. The vertex $o$ is an ordinary point of affine weight $1$, not the null vector $z$.

Let $I=\{x_1,\ldots,x_m\}$. The spanning-tree form of the new graph is $[(ox_1\ldots x_m)]^2$. It is the coefficient of this form that is extracted in the potential $u_{\widehat M}$.

> [!info] Fundamental matrix
> **Definition and metric identity.** Denote the matrix of inner products of residuals by $\mathbf{Fm}$ and call it the fundamental matrix of the internal problem. The isometry under contraction and the metric identity give
>
> $$
> \boxed{\mathrm{Fm}_{xy}=r_x\cdot r_y
> =\frac{u_{\widehat M}([v_x,v_y])}{u_{\widehat M}(e)}}
> \tag{5}
> $$

This is the positive definite Gram matrix of the residual basis. Its diagonal entry $\mathrm{Fm}_{xx}=r_x^2$ is the squared distance from $x$ to $\mathcal A_P$; in the contracted graph, it is the effective resistance between $x$ and $o$. Off-diagonal entries are inner products, not pairwise effective resistances.

For our path, identifying $a,b$ produces a triangle:

$$
\widehat L=2[v_x]^2+[v_y-v_x]^2+3[v_y]^2
$$

![[The Contracted Graph of the Dirichlet Problem.svg]]

*Figure 3. The selected vertices $a,b$ are identified as $o$. The contracted graph retains the metric of residuals. To compute influence, we separately retain which original selected vertex was the endpoint of each coupling.*

Set $T=[v_x\wedge v_y]^2=[(oxy)]^2$. Direct multiplication of forms gives

$$
\widehat M=e+\widehat L+11T
$$

The coefficient $11$ is the sum of the weights of the triangle's three spanning trees: $2\cdot1+2\cdot3+1\cdot3$. To calculate the potential of a grade-1 form, it is enough to multiply it by $\widehat L$ and extract the coefficient of $T$:

$$
\widehat L[v_x]^2=4T,\qquad
\widehat L[v_y]^2=3T,\qquad
\widehat L[v_x,v_y]=T
$$

For example, only $[v_y-v_x]^2$ contributes to the last product. Both exterior arguments, $(v_y-v_x)\wedge v_x$ and $(v_y-v_x)\wedge v_y$, equal $-v_x\wedge v_y$, so the product has positive sign. By (5),

$$
\mathbf{Fm}=\frac1{11}\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}
$$

In particular, $r_x^2=4/11$ and $r_y^2=3/11$. The geometric calculation gives the same numbers: $r_x^2=1/2-(1/2)^2/(11/6)=4/11$, $r_y^2=3/2-(3/2)^2/(11/6)=3/11$.

### From the internal metric to influence

Contraction preserves the sums $d_x^P$, but no longer distinguishes couplings to individual vertices in $P$. To recover the influence of each selected vertex, use the original coefficients $c_{yp}$.

**Theorem.** The bi-coordinates of projections and the influence coefficients are given by

$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
b_{xp}&=\sum_{y\in I}\mathrm{Fm}_{xy}c_{yp}\\
&=\sum_{y\in I}c_{yp}\frac{u_{\widehat M}([v_x,v_y])}{u_{\widehat M}(e)}
\end{aligned}}
\tag{6}
$$

> [!proof]- Proof using reciprocal bases
> By the lemma, $s_y\cdot r_x$ equals the internal coordinate of $r_x$ in position $y$: it is $1$ when $x=y$ and $0$ otherwise. The vectors $s_y$ lie in the orthogonal complement of $W_P$ and form the reciprocal basis to the $r_x$.
>
> The expansion of $r_x$ in this reciprocal basis is therefore
>
> $$
> r_x=\sum_{y\in I}(r_x\cdot r_y)s_y
> =\sum_{y\in I}\mathrm{Fm}_{xy}s_y
> $$
>
> The coefficient of a selected vertex $p$ on the left is $-b_{xp}$, since $r_x=x-q_x$. On the right, the coefficient of $p$ in each $s_y$ is $-c_{yp}$. Comparing these coefficients gives the first equality in (6); the second follows from (5). $\square$

For the path, the couplings to the selected vertices form the matrix

$$
\mathbf C=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf B=\mathbf{Fm}\,\mathbf C
=\frac1{11}\begin{pmatrix}8&3\\2&9\end{pmatrix}
$$

The rows of $\mathbf C$ correspond to $x,y$ and the columns to $a,b$. We have obtained the influence matrix directly from products of forms of the contracted graph and the original coupling coefficients. It agrees with both the solution of the equations and the geometric projections.

## Matrix notation for computation

Collect the internal equations into a system. Let $\mathbf A$ have rows and columns indexed by $I$, with entries

$$
A_{xx}=d_x=\sum_{j\in\mathcal V}c_{xj},\qquad
A_{xy}=-c_{xy}\quad(x\ne y)
$$

This is the internal principal block of the full Laplacian matrix. Its diagonal includes couplings to all vertices, including $P$. Thus $\mathbf A$ is not the Laplacian of the graph obtained by deleting the selected vertices and their couplings.

**Lemma.** The fundamental matrix inverts the internal block:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf{Fm}=\mathbf A^{-1},\qquad
\mathbf B=\mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf C}
\tag{7}
$$

> [!proof]- Proof
> In the expansion $s_x=\sum_jc_{xj}(x-a_j)$, replace each vertex by the sum of its projection and residual. For $p\in P$, the residual is zero, and the sum of the projected terms vanishes by the theorem proved above. We obtain
>
> $$
> s_x=\sum_{y\in I}A_{xy}r_y
> $$
>
> Take the inner product with $r_z$. The left-hand side is $1$ when $x=z$ and $0$ otherwise; the right-hand side is $\sum_y A_{xy}\mathrm{Fm}_{yz}$. Thus $\mathbf A\mathbf{Fm}$ is the identity matrix. The second equality in (7) is the matrix form of (6). $\square$

If $\mathbf f_I$ and $\mathbf f_P$ are the columns of internal and prescribed values, then

$$
\mathbf A\mathbf f_I=\mathbf C\mathbf f_P,\qquad
\mathbf f_I=\mathbf B\mathbf f_P
$$

In our example, $\mathbf A=\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-1&4\end{pmatrix}$, its determinant is $11$, and its inverse agrees with the previously calculated $\mathbf{Fm}$.

For a single set of prescribed values, it is enough to solve a linear system; the inverse need not be computed explicitly. Nor is a full expansion of the metric polyform required. Formula (6) establishes the algebraic and geometric meaning of the coefficients, but does not by itself promise a faster numerical algorithm.

## Nonnegativity and limitations

**Proposition.** In the graph model considered here,

$$
b_{xp}\ge0,\qquad \sum_{p\in P}b_{xp}=1,\qquad
\min_{p\in P}f_p\le f_x\le\max_{p\in P}f_p
\tag{8}
$$

> [!proof]- The maximum principle and properties of the coefficients
> If the function attains its maximum at an internal vertex, equality with the weighted average forces all its neighbors to have the same value. Along a path to a selected vertex, this maximum propagates to $P$. The same argument applies to the minimum. This proves the last part of (8).
>
> For data equal to $1$ at one selected vertex and $0$ at the others, the solution lies between $0$ and $1$. Its internal values form the corresponding column of $\mathbf B$, so $b_{xp}\ge0$. Constant prescribed values of $1$ extend to the constant function $1$, so every row of $\mathbf B$ sums to $1$. $\square$

Geometrically, $q_x$ lies in the convex hull of the selected vertices: it is a combination of them with nonnegative coefficients summing to $1$. For two selected vertices, the projection lies on the segment joining them. Nonnegativity is a property of this graph problem, not part of the general definition of bi-coordinates.

The coefficients need not be strictly positive. For example, on the path $x-p-q$ with $P=\{p,q\}$, the only internal equation gives $f_x=f_p$. The projection of $x$ is $p$, and the influence row is $(1,0)$. The equality $q_x=p$ does not mean that $x=p$.

With a single selected vertex, the solution is constant, and every influence row consists of a single unit entry. If all vertices are selected, there are no internal unknowns, and the prescribed values already determine the solution.

### Disconnected graphs

For nonnegative coupling coefficients, the solution is unique if and only if every connected component contains at least one selected vertex. The conditions and proof apply separately to each component.

In a component without selected vertices, the internal equations allow any constant value. For example, consider a graph consisting of two disconnected couplings $a-x$ and $b-y$, with only $a$ selected. Then $f_x=f_a$, while $f_b=f_y=t$ for arbitrary $t$. The internal block of the entire graph is singular in this case.

If every component has a selected vertex, the influence matrix is defined, and coefficients between different components are zero. Geometric projections are considered within each component. The PMG core does not define a finite resistance metric between different components, and (5) should be applied separately to their metric polyforms.

Positive coefficients on existing couplings are essential to the proofs of the minimum principle, the maximum principle, and nonnegative influence. For coefficients of either sign, the algebraic system may remain solvable, but these properties require separate verification.

## What comes next

We have found the values at internal vertices and described their projections. The next question is which graph on the selected vertices reproduces the original metric and the response to prescribed values. This is the problem of eliminating internal vertices, considered in [[Vertex Elimination and Induced Couplings]].

The isotropic extension was not needed here. All inner products were calculated for differences of points and residuals, that is, for affine vectors. The role of $z$ in the extended representation remains as established in the [[The Null Vector, Isotropic Representation, and the Boundary|note on the null vector]].

## Sources and status of the results

The Dirichlet problem, its variational principle, and the maximum principle are classical results [1]. This note provides proofs for the graph model used here. The word “harmonic” describes a function satisfying (1); bi-coordinates and di-coordinates retain their [[Projections, Bi-coordinates, and Di-coordinates|agreed definitions]].

The equality between influence coefficients and the bi-coordinates of projections is proved using the resistance inner product. Formulas (5)-(7) relate this construction to contraction, reciprocal bases, and polyform potentials. The internal research note “The Dirichlet Problem in Polyform Algebra” served as source material; its earlier identification of bi-coordinates with barycentric coordinates is not used here.

For comparison with [2], in the absence of loops and with our index ordering, $\mathbf B$ is the transpose of the authors' accompanying matrix $L_{ac}$. Their columns sum to $1$, whereas our rows do. The accompanying matrix and the response matrix are different objects; the latter will be considered when eliminating internal vertices.

For the weighted path, the solution, geometric projections, and potentials were calculated independently. Additional checks using exact rational arithmetic covered six cases: the weighted path, a cycle, a complete graph, a weighted graph with cycles, a single selected vertex, and an example with a zero influence coefficient. Solutions of the internal equations were compared with projections calculated from the Gram matrix of the original metric and with potentials extracted by direct exterior multiplication. Singularity of the internal block in the presence of a component without selected vertices was checked separately. The note contains no working conjectures.
