In the [[Spaces, Subspaces, and the Choice of a Basis|note on choosing a subspace]], points form an affine space, while lengths and inner products are defined on their differences. We now consider another representation of the same geometry: each point is assigned a linear element of a larger space so that the inner product of two representatives directly expresses the distance between the points.

To do this, we introduce a distinguished null vector. It will allow us to express affine weight as an inner product, represent affine subspaces by exterior products, and relate these products to the PMG boundary operator.

In this new representation, we must distinguish a point from its representative. Their affine combinations behave differently. This distinction will guide the construction.

## The original points and the extended space

We retain the notation for the spaces of points and vectors. The space $V$ is generated by formally independent vertices $a_1,\ldots,a_n$. The affine weight $\varepsilon$ is the sum of the coefficients. Vectors form the space $W=\ker\varepsilon$, while points form the set $\mathcal A=\{x\in V:\varepsilon(x)=1\}$. Here $\ker$ denotes the set of elements on which the functional is zero.

The space $W$ carries a positive definite metric. For a graph, this is the resistance metric of a connected undirected graph with positive coefficients for all existing couplings. Write $d$ for the dimension of $W$; in this graph model, $d=n-1$.

Choose $a_1$ as the origin. Every point has a unique expression $x=a_1+w$, where $w\in W$.

**Definition.** Adjoin two independent linear elements $o,z$ to $W$. Denote the resulting space by $\widetilde V$. Its elements have the form $\alpha o+w+\beta z$. Extend the inner product by the conditions

$$
o^2=z^2=0,\qquad o\cdot z=1,\qquad
o\cdot w=z\cdot w=0\quad(w\in W)
\tag{1}
$$

Here $U^2$ denotes the number $U\cdot U$. The letter $o$ denotes the representative of the chosen origin, not an orthocenter. For arbitrary elements of the extension, we obtain

$$
(\alpha o+w+\beta z)\cdot(\alpha' o+w'+\beta' z)
=w\cdot w'+\alpha\beta'+\beta\alpha'
$$

For example, $(o+z)^2=2$, while $(o-z)^2=-2$. The extended metric is therefore no longer positive definite. The elements $(o+z)/\sqrt2$ and $(o-z)/\sqrt2$ are orthogonal and have squares $1$ and $-1$. Together with the $d$ positive directions in $W$, they give **signature $(d+1,1)$**: $d+1$ positive directions and one negative direction. The dimension of $\widetilde V$ is $d+2$.

> [!info] A null vector and the zero element
> A nonzero element with zero square is called **isotropic**. The distinguished element $z\ne0$ with $z^2=0$ is called the null vector. It is not the zero element of the space: for example, $z\cdot o=1$, whereas the zero element has zero inner product with every element.

We retain “null vector” as the PMG term. It does not mean that $z$ belongs to the original positive definite space of affine vectors $W$.

## The isotropic representative of a point

To a point $x=a_1+w$, assign the element

$$
X(x)=o+w-\frac{w^2}{2}z
\tag{2}
$$

Conditions (1) immediately give $X(x)^2=0$ and $X(x)\cdot z=1$.

> [!info] Conditions for a point representative
> **Definition.** Normalized isotropic representatives of points are elements $X\in\widetilde V$ satisfying two conditions:
>
> $$
> X^2=0,\qquad X\cdot z=1
> $$

The first condition specifies isotropy; the second fixes the scale of the representative. The element $z$ itself does not satisfy the second condition and does not represent a finite point of the original space.

**Lemma.** Formula (2) gives all normalized isotropic representatives and preserves distances.

> [!proof]- Proof
> Write an arbitrary element as $X=\alpha o+w+\beta z$. The condition $X\cdot z=1$ gives $\alpha=1$, while $X^2=0$ gives $w^2+2\beta=0$. This is precisely (2).
>
> If $x=a_1+w$ and $y=a_1+v$, then
>
> $$
> X(x)\cdot X(y)=w\cdot v-\frac{w^2+v^2}{2}
> =-\frac{(w-v)^2}{2}
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> (X(y)-X(x))^2=-2X(x)\cdot X(y)=(y-x)^2
> $$
>
> The right-hand side is the squared distance in the original affine space. $\square$

### Example: the same path on three vertices

Consider the path $a-b-c$ with unit coupling coefficients. Set $u=b-a$ and $v=c-b$. In the resistance metric, $u^2=v^2=1$ and $u\cdot v=0$. Choose the origin at $a$ and denote the vertex representatives by capital letters:

$$
A=X(a)=o,\qquad
B=X(b)=o+u-\frac12z,\qquad
C=X(c)=o+u+v-z
$$

Their inner products are

$$
A\cdot B=B\cdot C=-\frac12,\qquad A\cdot C=-1
$$

These reproduce the effective resistances $R_{ab}=R_{bc}=1$ and $R_{ac}=2$. For example, $(C-A)^2=-2A\cdot C=2$.

A four-dimensional space of signature $(3,1)$ is needed for the entire resistance plane. The two-dimensional geometry of points has not thereby become four-dimensional: the two conditions on a representative leave two independent parameters.

## Linear combinations and correction along the null vector

The map $x\mapsto X(x)$ is nonlinear. Thus $x=\sum_i x_i a_i$ with $\sum_i x_i=1$ does not imply $X(x)=\sum_i x_iX(a_i)$.

To distinguish these expressions, write $A_i=X(a_i)$ and introduce the **linear replacement of basis vertices**:

$$
\widetilde p=\sum_i p_iA_i\quad\text{for}\quad p=\sum_i p_i a_i
$$

The tilde denotes the linear replacement $a_i\mapsto A_i$. We will also apply it to exterior products and forms by replacing each argument. The elements $A_i,z$ are independent. Indeed, in $\sum_i p_iA_i+\beta z=0$, the coefficient of $o$ first gives $\sum_i p_i=0$. Independence of the differences $a_i-a_1$ for $i\ne1$ then gives all $p_i=0$ and $\beta=0$.

For an affine point $x$, the element $\widetilde x$ already has weight $1$, but its square may be nonzero. We can correct this.

> [!info] Isotropic correction
> **Lemma.** If $p\cdot z=1$, the unique element of the form $p+\lambda z$ with zero square is
>
> $$
> p-\frac{p^2}{2}z
> \tag{3}
> $$

> [!proof]- Proof
> Under conditions (1),
>
> $$
> (p+\lambda z)^2=p^2+2\lambda
> $$
>
> Thus $\lambda=-p^2/2$. The inner product with $z$ remains $1$. $\square$

In particular,

$$
X(x)=\widetilde x-\frac{\widetilde x^2}{2}z
$$

To verify this equality, observe that for $x=a_1+w$, the element $\widetilde x$ has the form $o+w+\beta z$. Its square is $w^2+2\beta$, and correction (3) gives formula (2).

### The midpoint and the entire line

On the line through $a,c$, consider the points $x(t)=(1-t)a+tc$. Their linear representatives are $p(t)=(1-t)A+tC$. Since $A^2=C^2=0$ and $A\cdot C=-1$, we obtain

$$
p(t)^2=-2t(1-t),\qquad
X(x(t))=(1-t)A+tC+t(1-t)z
$$

For the midpoint $m=(a+c)/2$, this gives

$$
\widetilde m=\frac{A+C}{2},\qquad
\widetilde m^2=-\frac12,\qquad
X(m)=\frac{A+C}{2}+\frac14z
$$

Thus the midpoint of the representatives and the representative of the midpoint differ by $z/4$.

![[Isotropic.svg]]

*The left panel shows points in the original resistance plane. The right panel uses the affine plane of expressions $(1-t)A+tC+\eta z$: the horizontal coordinate is $t$, and the vertical coordinate is the coefficient $\eta$ of $z$. The line $\eta=0$ contains the linear combinations $p(t)$; the parabola $\eta=t(1-t)$ contains the isotropic representatives. This is a coordinate diagram, not a depiction of Euclidean lengths and angles in the extension.*

All real values of $t$ describe the original line. The condition $0\le t\le1$ selects the segment. Formula (2), or direct expansion of the inner product, gives

$$
(X(x(t))-X(x(s)))^2=2(t-s)^2
$$

Distance is determined by this equality, not by arc length along the drawn parabola. Correction (3) is not an orthogonal projector either: it is nonlinear, and the orthogonal projection formula involving division by $z^2$ cannot be used for the null direction $z$.

## Affine weight and the space of displacements

Every element of the extension has a unique expression $U=\sum_i u_iA_i+\beta z$. Extend affine weight to the larger space by

$$
\widetilde\varepsilon(U)=z\cdot U=\sum_i u_i
\tag{4}
$$

The coefficient of $z$ itself does not enter this sum. On linear images of elements of $V$, equation (4) agrees with the original weight: $\widetilde\varepsilon(\widetilde p)=\varepsilon(p)$.

For example, $z\cdot(2A-B+3z)=1$, while $z\cdot(C-A+3z)=0$. The first equality does not imply isotropy: the square of $2A-B+3z$ is $8$.

Write

$$
z^\perp=\{U\in\widetilde V:z\cdot U=0\}
$$

for the set of elements orthogonal to $z$. The symbol $\perp$ denotes orthogonality. The element $z$ itself belongs to $z^\perp$ and is orthogonal to all its elements. The metric on this subspace is therefore degenerate, although the metric on the whole of $\widetilde V$ is nondegenerate.

For $U,V\in z^\perp$, we have

$$
(U+\alpha z)\cdot(V+\beta z)=U\cdot V
$$

For example, $C-A=u+v-z$ and $u+v$ differ by a multiple of $z$, but both have square $2$. Moreover,

$$
X(m)-A=\frac{u+v}{2}-\frac14z,\qquad
\frac{C-A}{2}=\frac{u+v}{2}-\frac12z
$$

These elements are not equal. Nevertheless, they represent the same displacement and have identical inner products with every element of $z^\perp$.

**Definition.** Regard $U$ and $U+\alpha z$ in $z^\perp$ as representatives of the same displacement. The space of these classes is denoted by $z^\perp/\mathbb Rz$, where $\mathbb Rz$ means all real multiples of $z$. This construction is called a quotient space.

In our model, every element of $z^\perp$ has the form $w+\beta z$. Its class is uniquely determined by $w\in W$, and inner products of classes agree with inner products in $W$. Hence

$$
z^\perp/\mathbb Rz\simeq W
$$

Here $\simeq$ denotes a linear correspondence preserving the metric. Both this space of classes and $W$ have dimension $d$, while $z^\perp$ has dimension $d+1$.

The linear image $\widetilde W$ of the original $W$ also provides one representative of each class. For $w=\sum_i w_i a_i$ with coefficient sum zero, its image has the form $\widetilde w=w+\beta z$ for some scalar $\beta$. Therefore, $\widetilde w\cdot\widetilde v=w\cdot v$. This is the precise sense in which the original vector metric is preserved.

## Where the flat geometry appears, and which normal is involved

The condition $X\cdot z=1$ defines an affine hyperplane in $\widetilde V$. Its direction space is $z^\perp$. Thus $z$ is a normal to this hyperplane: it is orthogonal to every direction in it.

Because $z^2=0$, the normal also belongs to the direction space of the hyperplane. This would be impossible for a nonzero normal in positive definite geometry; here it is consistent with the degeneracy of the metric on $z^\perp$.

Point representatives lie at the intersection of this hyperplane with the **null cone**, the set of elements $X$ satisfying $X^2=0$. The intersection itself is not an affine plane in the ambient space. Nevertheless, the distance formula shows that it represents the original flat Euclidean geometry. The curvature of the parabola in the coordinate diagram is not the intrinsic curvature of that geometry.

### A normal to a selected direction need not be $z$

In the example with $A,C$, set $N=-A-C$. Then

$$
N\cdot A=N\cdot C=1,\qquad
N\cdot(C-A)=0,\qquad N^2=-2
$$

The element $N$ is normal to the selected direction $C-A$, but not to the entire normalization hyperplane. Indeed,

$$
N\cdot X(x(t))=1-2t(1-t)
$$

This inner product varies along the isotropic representation of the line, whereas $z\cdot X(x(t))=1$ for every $t$.

Thus the question “Does the normal coincide with the null vector?” requires us to specify the subspace or section to which the normal refers. In this note, flat geometry is specified by normalization with respect to $z$. Sections with other normals and the geometry of spheres are considered separately; a normal with negative square for a selected set does not by itself change the geometry of the surrounding space.

## Affine subspaces in exterior form

The exterior product, denoted by $\wedge$, is defined for all linear elements of $\widetilde V$. Antisymmetry gives

$$
z\wedge(p+\lambda z)=z\wedge p
$$

This equality does not use the metric. In particular, isotropic correction (3) leaves the exterior product with $z$ unchanged:

$$
z\wedge X(x)=z\wedge\widetilde x
$$

For example, $z\wedge X(m)=z\wedge(A+C)/2$, although $X(m)\ne(A+C)/2$.

### A point and a line

Consider the nonzero exterior element $z\wedge A$. By the [[Spaces, Subspaces, and the Choice of a Basis#Specifying a subspace by an exterior element|criterion for membership in a subspace]], the condition $X\wedge z\wedge A=0$ means that $X=\alpha A+\beta z$. If we also require $X\cdot z=1$ and $X^2=0$, we obtain $\alpha=1$ and $\beta=0$. The only finite point represented is $a$.

For the line through $a,c$, use $z\wedge A\wedge C$. The condition

$$
X\wedge z\wedge A\wedge C=0
$$

means that $X=\alpha A+\beta C+\eta z$. Normalization gives $\alpha+\beta=1$. Setting $\beta=t$ and using $X^2=0$, we obtain $\eta=t(1-t)$. These are precisely all the representatives of the line found above.

If we retain only $A\wedge C$, the normalized isotropic elements in its linear span have the form $p(t)$ and satisfy $-2t(1-t)=0$. Only $A$ and $C$ remain. Thus the exterior representation of a line in the isotropic model also requires $z$.

### A subspace of arbitrary dimension

Let $q_0,\ldots,q_k$ be affinely independent, and let $Q_i=X(q_i)$. Then the element

$$
F=z\wedge Q_0\wedge\cdots\wedge Q_k
$$

is nonzero and specifies their $k$-dimensional affine hull as follows: take the elements $X$ satisfying $X\wedge F=0$, $X^2=0$, and $X\cdot z=1$ simultaneously.

> [!proof]- Proof
> Independence of $z,Q_0,\ldots,Q_k$ follows from affine independence of the $q_i$: in a linear dependence, the coefficients of the $Q_i$ must first sum to zero and then give a zero combination of spatial differences. They must all vanish.
>
> The condition $X\wedge F=0$ means that $X=\sum_i\lambda_iQ_i+\eta z$. Normalization gives $\sum_i\lambda_i=1$, and isotropy uniquely determines $\eta$ by (3). The spatial part of this $X$ corresponds to the point $\sum_i\lambda_iq_i$. Conversely, this procedure gives the representative of every point in the hull. $\square$

For example, $z\wedge A\wedge B\wedge C$ specifies the entire resistance plane of our path. In its minimal four-dimensional extension, $X\wedge F=0$ holds automatically by dimension; the two conditions for a point representative remain. The triangle is selected by the additional inequalities $\lambda_i\ge0$. The exterior product itself does not encode these inequalities.

## The boundary operator as contraction with $z$

In the PMG core, the boundary operator is defined by affine weight and requires no metric. In the extension, the same weight has the metric expression (4).

**Definition.** On an exterior product of linear elements $U_1,\ldots,U_k\in\widetilde V$, set

$$
\partial(U_1\wedge\cdots\wedge U_k)
=\sum_{i=1}^k(-1)^{i-1}(z\cdot U_i)
U_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{U_i}\wedge\cdots\wedge U_k
\tag{5}
$$

The hat over a factor means that it is omitted. The operation extends by linearity; on scalars, set $\partial1=0$. Removing one factor at a time with these weights and signs is called **contraction with the functional** $\widetilde\varepsilon$, or, here, contraction with $z$.

For point representatives, $z\cdot A_i=1$. Thus the formula agrees with the PMG boundary operator after the linear replacement of vertices. For example,

$$
\partial(A\wedge C)=C-A
$$

$$
\partial(A\wedge B\wedge C)
=B\wedge C-A\wedge C+A\wedge B
=(B-A)\wedge(C-A)
$$

The last expression is the linear image of the boundary $(abc)$ in the original space $V$. Parentheses still denote an algebraic boundary, not the selected vertex set of a Dirichlet problem.

On an element of grade $1$, we have $\partial U=z\cdot U$. For example, $\partial(C-A+3z)=0$. The boundary of a scalar is zero, so even for a pair of points we have $\partial^2(A\wedge C)=\partial(C-A)=1-1=0$.

In general, each removal of a pair of factors occurs twice with opposite signs. Hence $\partial^2=0$. For a homogeneous exterior element $S$ of grade $k$, expanding (5) also gives

$$
\partial(S\wedge T)=\partial S\wedge T+(-1)^kS\wedge\partial T
$$

In particular, since $\partial z=0$,

$$
\partial(z\wedge T)=-z\wedge\partial T
$$

For example, $\partial(z\wedge A)=-z$, while $\partial(z\wedge A\wedge C)=-z\wedge(C-A)$.

> [!note] Multiplication and the boundary
> Exterior multiplication by $z$ raises the grade by one. The boundary operator lowers it by one. They are related by an identity but are not the same operation. The operator $\partial$ itself does not require a null vector: the affine-weight functional is sufficient to define it. The condition $z^2=0$ belongs to the chosen metric realization.

## Metric evaluations of exterior elements and forms

The inner product extends to exterior elements of the larger space just as in [[Metric of Higher-Grade Objects]]: the inner product of two decomposable elements of the same grade is the determinant of the matrix of pairwise inner products of their factors. The rule extends to sums by bilinearity.

In particular, the square of a decomposable element is the determinant of its Gram matrix, the matrix of inner products of its factors with one another. This numerical evaluation may be negative; the notation $S^2=S\cdot S$ here does not denote the square of a positive norm.

For example,

$$
(z\wedge A)^2=
\det\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}=-1
$$

For the end vertices of our path, an independent calculation gives

$$
(z\wedge A\wedge C)^2=
\det\begin{pmatrix}
0&1&1\\
1&0&-1\\
1&-1&0
\end{pmatrix}=-2
$$

The negative sign does not mean that the squared distance between $a,c$ is negative. The connection with distance is given by the following identity.

> [!info] The null vector and metric evaluation of the boundary
> **Theorem.** For every homogeneous exterior element $S$ of the extended space,
>
> $$
> (z\wedge S)^2=-(\partial S)^2
> \tag{6}
> $$

> [!proof]- Proof
> Expanding the determinant along a row gives
>
> $$
> (z\wedge B)\cdot C=B\cdot\partial C
> $$
>
> for elements $B,C$ of grades $k-1,k$. For decomposable elements, the expansion coefficients are exactly $(-1)^{i-1}(z\cdot C_i)$ from (5); the equality then extends by bilinearity. Apply it twice:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> (z\wedge S)^2
> &=S\cdot\partial(z\wedge S)\\
> &=-S\cdot(z\wedge\partial S)\\
> &=-(\partial S)\cdot(\partial S)
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> For scalar grade, both sides of (6) are directly zero. $\square$

If $S=A\wedge C$, the right-hand side is $-(C-A)^2=-2$, in agreement with the direct determinant calculation. If $S=A\wedge B\wedge C$, then

$$
(z\wedge A\wedge B\wedge C)^2
=-\bigl((B-A)\wedge(C-A)\bigr)^2=-1
$$

Here $B-A=u-z/2$ and $C-A=u+v-z$. Their squares are $1,2$, and their inner product is $1$. The square of the boundary is therefore $1\cdot2-1^2=1$. This is the squared area of the parallelogram; the triangle itself has area $1/2$.

### Passing to PMG forms

Formal forms $[S,T]$ can now be constructed with exterior arguments from $\widetilde V$. Their product follows the same rule as before:

$$
[S,T][P,Q]=[S\wedge P,T\wedge Q]
$$

As before, $[S]^2$ means $[S,S]$. The form itself is distinct from the number $S^2$.

For example, the form $[z]^2$ is nonzero: its definition imposes only bilinearity relations, and $z$ is a nonzero basis element of the extension. Yet its numerical metric evaluation is $z^2=0$. We also have

$$
([z]^2)^2=[z\wedge z]^2=0
$$

The last equality follows from antisymmetry of the exterior product and holds for the form of every linear element, not only an isotropic one.

Multiplication by another form can produce a nonzero evaluation:

$$
[z]^2[A\wedge C]^2=[z\wedge A\wedge C]^2
\quad\longmapsto\quad -2
$$

The arrow denotes the passage from a formal form to its numerical metric evaluation. Thus a form with zero evaluation can have a product with nonzero evaluation. Metric evaluation of a product is not multiplication of the numerical evaluations of its factors.

For mixed arguments, the same proof gives

$$
(z\wedge S)\cdot(z\wedge T)
=-(\partial S)\cdot(\partial T)
$$

This applies to $S,T$ of the same grade and extends by linearity to forms and polyforms. It is an equality of **metric evaluations**, not an equality of the forms $[z\wedge S,z\wedge T]$ and $-[\partial S,\partial T]$: their grades differ.

### What is preserved from the graph's metric polyform

The linear replacement $a_i\mapsto A_i$ takes forms on $W$ to forms on $\widetilde W$. It preserves exterior products and the metric of the arguments, and therefore preserves all metric evaluations constructed earlier. In particular, it takes the Laplacian to

$$
\widetilde L=\sum_{i<j}c_{ij}[A_j-A_i]^2
$$

Here $c_{ij}$ is the coupling coefficient. The exponential is carried over with it: $\widetilde M_G=\exp\widetilde L$. The spanning-tree form and the functional extracting its coefficient must be carried over by the same linear replacement. The normalized potentials of the original forms then remain unchanged.

In our example, the evaluation of $[C-A]^2$ equals the normalized potential of $[(ac)]^2$ in the original graph and is $2$. Identity (6) expresses the same value through the extended form $[z\wedge A\wedge C]^2$, with a minus sign.

This does not assert that the core metric identity holds for all forms on $\widetilde V$. Forms whose arguments contain $z$ or point representatives have been assigned a numerical evaluation here by the extended bilinear metric. Expressing this evaluation through a separate metric polyform of the extension requires an additional definition.

## Scope and the role of the construction

The construction uses the positive definite metric on the original $W$. For a connected graph, this is provided by the published PMG core. For a disconnected graph, the model can be constructed separately on each component; the original resistance construction does not provide finite distances between different components.

The exterior representation of a subspace requires affinely independent generators. For example, $a,m,c$ lie on one line. Although $A,X(m),C$ are linearly independent in the three-dimensional span of $A,C,z$, the product $z\wedge A\wedge X(m)\wedge C$ vanishes. It does not specify a plane: $z\wedge A\wedge C$ suffices for this line.

Explicitly introducing $z$ is not necessary for calculating effective resistances, finding projections in $W$, or solving the Dirichlet problem. Here it serves to give a consistent description of point representatives, affine weight, exterior representations of subspaces, and the metric realization of the boundary. The geometry of spheres and other sections of the null cone remains a separate continuation.

[[Projections, Bi-coordinates, and Di-coordinates]] applies this distinction to coordinates: an affine point, its linear image, and its isotropic representative have different expressions.

## Sources and status of the results

The definitions of the original points, vectors, boundary, and forms are taken from the [[Core specification|core specification]]. The construction of the extension, the correction of representatives, the criterion for the exterior representation of an affine subspace, and identity (6) are proved in this note. The numerical examples are obtained by direct calculation; the determinant for $z\wedge A\wedge C$ is calculated independently of (6).

Additional checks were carried out using exact rational arithmetic in the four-dimensional model of the example: point representatives, the midpoint correction, distance formulas, and determinant evaluations were verified. The bilinear version of (6) was checked on all 70 pairs of exterior basis elements of the same grade. This finite-dimensional check serves to verify signs and does not replace the general proof. The note contains no working conjectures.

The internal Russian notes “Isotropic Representation of Points and the Null Vector”, “Sphere Normals and Lorentzian Geometry”, and “The Null Vector: Exterior Products and the Boundary” were checked during preparation. Their notation has been aligned here with the published core: the numerical evaluation $S^2$ is distinguished from the formal form $[S]^2$, and the original point from its isotropic representative.

The isotropic representation of Euclidean points and the exterior representation of lines are known constructions in conformal geometric algebra. The correspondence was checked against [H. Li, D. Hestenes, A. Rockwood, *Generalized Homogeneous Coordinates for Computational Geometry*](https://davidhestenes.net/geocalc/pdf/CompGeom-ch2.pdf), §2.3, equations (2.25), (2.26), and (2.32)–(2.34). That source uses the normalization $X\cdot e=-1$; our notation corresponds to $z=-e$ and $o=e_0$. The established model is used here for an exposition in the language of PMG. The geometric product of Clifford algebra is not identified with the PMG product of forms.
